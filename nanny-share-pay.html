How to split nanny pay in a nanny share

By Ingrid Hartman, who runs a nanny share in Salt Lake City and built OurNanny · Updated October 31, 2026

The short answer Most nanny shares pay the nanny a higher hourly rate for two children than for one, and each family pays about two-thirds of the nanny's solo rate. When schedules differ, split by the hour: hours with both children are billed at the two-child rate and shared, and hours with one child are billed at the one-child rate to that child's family.

What is a fair nanny share rate?

A nanny caring for two children from two families is doing more work and taking on two employers, so the total hourly rate goes up, while each family's cost goes down. The common rule of thumb is that each family pays about two-thirds of the nanny's solo rate. Nanny Lane's guide gives the example of a $16 an hour nanny: each family pays about $10 and the nanny earns about $20 an hour.

For example, a nanny who charges $24 an hour for one child would earn about $32 an hour in a two-family share, with each family paying about $16. Some shares land closer to three-quarters of the solo rate per family. Rates vary a lot by city, so start from what nannies charge where you live, then agree the share rate with your nanny before you start.

It helps to write down two numbers, not one: the nanny's total hourly rate when one child is there and the total when two (or more) are there. Every split below follows from those two numbers.

How do you split pay when both children are there all day?

Split the two-child rate evenly per child. If the nanny earns $32 an hour for two children, each family pays $16 for every hour both children are together. For a 40-hour week that's $640 per family and $1,280 for the nanny.

How do you split pay when your schedules don't line up?

This is where most shares get stuck. The fairest method is to split the day into stretches at every drop-off and pick-up, then price each stretch by how many children are there:

  1. Mark when each child arrives and leaves.
  2. For each stretch, use the nanny's total rate for that number of children.
  3. Split that stretch's cost evenly across the children who were there.
  4. Add up each family's stretches for the pay period.

Worked example: the nanny charges $25 an hour for one child and $32 an hour for two. Family A's child is there 9 to 5. Family B's child is there 9 to 3.

What if one child is sick or on vacation?

Decide this in writing before the first week, because it comes up fast. The usual options are:

  • The absent family keeps paying its share. The nanny's income stays steady, which is the same promise as guaranteed hours in a one-family job. The other family pays its normal share too.
  • The family that's there pays the one-child rate. Their cost goes up for that day, and the absent family pays nothing.
  • A mix: a set number of paid sick and vacation days per family, after which the one-child rate applies.

Whatever you pick, the nanny shouldn't earn less because a child stayed home. She held the time for both families.

Who pays for overtime and extra hours?

The family that asks for the extra time pays for it. If one family needs the nanny until 7, that family pays the one-child rate for those hours, or overtime if the nanny goes past 40 hours in the week. If both families need the extra time, split it the same way as the normal week.

What about taxes and payroll in a nanny share?

In most cases each family in a share is the nanny's employer for the part of the pay it covers, so each family usually runs its own payroll. Payroll services for household employers handle shares and can set this up. Whatever you use, the per-family totals above are the numbers you'd give it.
